# Story craft

Hold a story to six tests of tension. They serve three jobs: diagnosing an existing story or
scene, planning new material, and writing or revising the piece itself. Only what you hand back
differs. When no story or scene is named, ask which one to work on.

The tests work as one. The entry drops the audience beside a problem that forces a choice. Opposed
wants make that choice a fight, between people or inside one of them. What the audience knows, and
what it learns later, changes what the choice means. The choice exposes the character's inner
story.

## The six tests

**In Medias Res: enter an action already underway.** Start as late as the audience can still
follow, inside an action whose outcome matters. Let context arrive in passing, through what people
do and want, instead of ahead of the action. Leave early too: end on the turn, before the
wind-down or a summary of what it meant. Ask what is already happening when we arrive, what would
be lost by arriving later, and where the last necessary moment is.

**The Bear at the Door: a problem that forces action now.** This is Jerome Stern's shape: the
problem is significant, it is pressing, and this character has to act on it. The bear alone is only
a threat. The tension comes from what makes acting hard for this particular person, so name that
resistance. Their choice fits who they have been shown to be, and it raises the tension instead of
releasing it: each solution breeds the next problem, and the ending is rarely a plain win or loss.
The act that settles the problem is the character's own, never a rescue or an accident. Ask what
is at the door, why it cannot wait, what in this character resists acting, and what their choice
makes worse.

**Opposed wants: someone stands in the way.** Everyone in a scene wants something now, and at
least two of those wants cannot both be met. The two may belong to two people, or to one person
who cannot have both, so that any choice costs them the other. Allies who agree leave only the
enemy to supply conflict, and a person who only helps or explains has no part to play: give them a
priority that collides with someone's, or take them out. Ask who wants what, from whom, and whose
want blocks it.

**Dramatic irony: decide who knows what, and when.** Every fact that matters reaches the audience
either with the character who discovers it or ahead of a character who is walking toward it.
Choose on purpose. What the characters already know and act on, the audience knows too. What a
character has yet to discover is held until they discover it. Give it to the audience ahead of
everyone only when that makes each scene before the discovery tenser than the discovery itself
would be. Decide it for the whole audience at once. A side story, a prequel, an ad, or an episode
met out of turn can hand the fact to some of the audience and not to the rest, and no scene can be
built for both, so settle which way it goes and close the other route. Ask who knows this now, who
does not, whether the whole audience is in the same position, and what its knowledge does to the
next scene.

**Retrospective tension: a discovery changes what earlier behavior meant.** A reveal earns its
place when it sends the audience back to a moment they have already seen, gives that moment a new
meaning, and forces a decision in the present. The earlier moment has to have been shown, and has
to make sense both before and after. New context complicates what came before; it does not cancel
it. Ask which earlier moment this changes, and what choice it forces now. Showing the outcome first
and then telling how it came about is a different device, a choice about the order of telling, and
these tests do not ask for it.

**Plot and inner story: each one moves the other.** A character's inner story has three parts: a
starting habit, a craving, and a direction of change. Every outside event forces a choice that
tests the habit or the craving, and every inner shift shows as an action that costs something.
Nobody announces their craving or their change; it shows in what they do. Establish the habit
before changing it. Early changes are partial and costly; later choices show whether the change
holds. A stretch with only events, or only feelings, is a weak point.

## Fit and size

Use the tests that fit the piece, and say in one line which you set aside and why. Forcing a
threat and a twist into every scene makes melodrama. Check plot against inner story always. Check
the entry, the pressing problem, and opposed wants on anything that opens or drives a unit. Check
dramatic irony wherever a fact that matters is known to some and not to others, among the
characters or among the audience, and retrospective tension only where something is revealed.

Scale each test to the piece, from a whole story down to an episode, a scene, or a thirty-second
ad. A scene has its own late entry and its own pressing problem, each a part of the story's larger
one. Follow the project's own form, whether prose, script, cutscene, or game narrative. The tests
work inside any story shape, whether three acts, a hero's journey, or none, and they do not pick
one for the piece.

## Story facts

Read the project's story documents first: the story so far, the cast, recorded decisions, and the
piece itself. When diagnosing, you may challenge a recorded decision; present that as a named
finding for the user to accept or reject. When writing, recorded decisions bind: keep to them and
flag the clash. Never write new story facts into the project's documents unasked.

When a piece needs a character's inner story and nothing defines it, infer the missing habit,
craving, or direction, say so in one line, carry on, and report the gap as a weak point. When the
work is an interview with the user, raise both cases as questions instead.

## What to hand back

- **Diagnosis:** the weak points, ordered by how much each hurts the story. For each, say where it
  is, which test it fails, what the audience loses, and one concrete change. Give no scores.
- **Plan:** the decisions that make the piece pass: the action it enters on, the problem and the
  resistance it meets, who stands in whose way, who knows what and when, what is revealed and what
  that changes, and how each beat tests the inner story.
- **Draft:** the text first, in the file alone when the user asked for one. Then at most five
  lines on where each test is met or set aside. Keep that note in the conversation and out of the
  story file.
