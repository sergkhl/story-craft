# Techniques: how each test fails, and where to see it work

Read this when a test's verdict is unclear, or when the user asks why a finding matters. The works
named below show a mechanism. Never borrow their content, tone, or plot.

These notes give away turns in Attack on Titan, Cowboy Bebop, Death Note, Neon Genesis Evangelion,
Final Fantasy VIII, and Frieren: Beyond Journey's End.

## In Medias Res

It fails when:

- The opening action has no consequence, or nobody the audience has a reason to watch. Noise is
  not a story already underway.
- The skipped setup comes straight back as a flashback or an explanation, so the late start bought
  nothing.
- The audience cannot tell who wants what. Confusion is not intrigue.
- The piece starts early to establish the world: waking up, travelling, arriving.
- The piece runs on after its turn: people wind down, travel home, or tell each other what it
  meant.

See it work:

- *Final Fantasy VIII* opens inside the duel between Squall and Seifer. Who they are and why they
  fight arrives afterwards, in the infirmary and the classroom.
- *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, "Angel Attack": an Angel is already advancing on the city and the
  army is already losing when Shinji arrives.

## The Bear at the Door

It fails when:

- Something is wrong but nothing has to be done about it. Stern's example is a depressed
  character, who can stay that way for a long time.
- The threat is real but anyone could face it. With no resistance inside the character, the piece
  is only action.
- The choice comes from nowhere: nothing earlier showed that the character could do this.
- The choice settles everything. The tension is released and the next scene has nothing to pay
  for.
- Someone or something else settles it: a rescue arrives, or a coincidence removes the threat.
- The ending is a plain win or a plain loss.

See it work:

- *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, "Angel Attack": the Angel is overhead, and if Shinji refuses, the
  wounded Rei is sent out in his place. What makes it hard is his father and his fear. Getting in
  the robot settles nothing.
- *Death Note*, "Confrontation": L's broadcast dares Kira to act. Light's pride answers, and the
  answer tells L which region he lives in. The solution is the next problem.

## Opposed wants

It fails when:

- Allies agree with each other and only the enemy opposes them.
- The disagreement is about facts: one person explains and the other learns. That is exposition
  dressed as a scene.
- People bicker with nothing at stake. Friction is not opposition unless someone can lose.
- A character is there to help, to listen, or to supply information.
- An inner conflict is named but never forces a choice. The person is torn and loses nothing.

See it work:

- *Attack on Titan*, "Midnight Sun": one dose and two dying people. Eren and Mikasa want it for
  Armin, Floch wants it for Erwin, and both sides are right. Levi holds both wants himself: the
  commander the cause needs, and the friend who has earned his rest. Whatever he chooses costs him
  the other.
- *Death Note*: Light and L work the same case in the same room, allies on paper, each needing the
  other close and each working toward the other's defeat.
- *Cowboy Bebop*: on most jobs Jet wants it done properly, Faye wants the money, and Spike wants
  something he will not say. The bounty is rarely the conflict.

## Dramatic irony

It fails when:

- The story hides what its own people already know. They are not surprised, so the audience is
  only confused.
- The audience is ahead and nothing rides on the gap. Nobody can be hurt by what they do not know.
- The audience stays ahead for so long that the characters seem slow, and each discovery scene
  repeats what the audience has had from the start.
- Part of the audience has the fact and part does not, because a trailer, a side story, or the
  order of release gave it away.
- The gap closes off stage, or at no cost.

See it work:

- *Death Note*: the audience knows who Kira is from the first episode, because Light does. Every
  scene with L, and every scene with Light's father, runs on that.
- *Attack on Titan*, the Marley arc: the audience recognizes the wounded soldier Falco has
  befriended before Falco does, and watches him lead Reiner down to the basement.

## Retrospective tension

It fails when:

- The reveal changes nothing now. It rewrites the past and no one has to decide anything.
- The earlier moment was never shown, or only makes sense once the secret is known.
- The reveal cancels what came before: it was a dream, he was innocent all along. Earlier stakes
  stop counting, and the audience stops trusting the story.

See it work:

- *Attack on Titan*, "Warrior": a confession on the wall turns years of comradeship into something
  else, and Eren has to answer it on the spot.
- *Frieren: Beyond Journey's End*: after Himmel's death, each memory of the old journey means more
  than it did when it happened, and each one steers what Frieren does next with Fern and Stark.

## Plot and inner story

It fails when:

- Events happen to people who could be swapped for others. Nothing tests anyone.
- People talk about feelings and nothing is risked.
- A character says what they crave or how they have changed. The audience is told the inner story
  instead of watching it.
- A change holds the first time it appears, at no cost. One rescue becomes forgiveness; one speech
  completes an arc.
- A habit changes before the audience has seen it at work.

See it work:

- *Neon Genesis Evangelion*: episodes 25 and 26 tell the ending as inner story alone, and *The End
  of Evangelion* tells the same ending with the events put back. Study them as a pair.
- *Frieren: Beyond Journey's End*: small errands, each of which makes Frieren choose between her
  habit of moving on and her wish to have known people better.
