# Cutscene craft

Hold a game's cutscenes and spoken lines to nine tests. They serve three jobs: diagnosing a
script, planning a cutscene, and writing or redrafting the lines. Only what you hand back differs.
When no scene or set of lines is named, ask which one to work on.

The tests work as one. A cutscene takes the controls away, so it has to give the player something
that playing couldn't. Its pictures carry whatever can be seen, which leaves the lines to do what
pictures can't: one person working on another, who answers. What each of them holds back gives the
lines their weight, and the way each of them talks tells them apart. Through all of it the game
has to keep moving, so a scene or a line that only holds the player up is cut.

## The cutscene

**Earn the cutscene: take the controls away only for what play can't give.** A cutscene stops the
player playing. Before writing one, try the cheaper ways in order: let the player do it, let them
see or hear it while they play, and only then stop the game to show it. What a cutscene can give
is an event that has to happen one way, a view the player couldn't have had, a turn that isn't
theirs to make, or a reward and a breath after a hard stretch. It never shows their character
doing what they could have done themselves, losing what they just won, or choosing what no player
would. It grows out of what the player just did, and comes when their hands are easing off the
controls. Make it as short as its turn allows. The Game Narrative Toolbox gives thirty seconds as
the general rule, and a longer one has to keep paying the player for its length. Players skip
cutscenes and half-watch them, so whatever they must know in order to play on is also given
outside it. Ask what this gives the player that playing couldn't, what they were doing just before
and what they do next, and what they must still know if they skip it.

**Picture first: the picture carries whatever can be seen.** Lay the scene out with no lines
first: what the audience sees happen, beat by beat. With the sound off they should still follow
who wants what and what changed. A line earns its place by adding what the picture can't show: a
want, a refusal, a fact that is out of sight, or a meaning that runs against the picture. A line
that says what the picture shows is cut, even when someone in the scene hasn't seen it: the
audience has. Let such a line start from what was seen, with the question it raises or the order
it calls for. Where a look, a gesture or an act can answer, let it. Silence is a line too, but a
pause only counts where pauses are rare. Say what each picture has to carry (what happens, who is
in it, what must be noticed, about how long it holds) and leave the lens, the angle and the cut to
the storyboard. Ask what the audience understands with the sound off, which lines only say what is
on screen, and which answer could be a look or an act.

**Wants and a turn: the scene gives the lines something to do.** Lines can't be mended in a scene
that has nothing for them to do, and rewording them only makes them plainer and worse. So check
the scene before the words. Each person in it wants something from another right now, something
that would end the scene if they got it, and at least two of those wants collide. By the end,
something at stake has changed for better or worse. That change is the turn. It comes neither in
the first exchange, which leaves the rest as aftermath, nor so late that the audience has seen it
coming. On the way to it the scene moves in beats, each one act and its answer, each doing
something the last didn't. The same act in new words is the same beat. Where a scene has no
colliding wants or no turn, say so as the first finding, then do what can be done for the lines. A
scene's larger structure (its entry, its pressure, who knows what) is story-craft's subject when
that skill is installed. Ask whose wants collide, what is different at the end and in which beat
it changes, and which beats repeat.

## The lines

**Act and answer: every line does something to someone, and is answered.** A line is something one
person does to another to get what they want: a dare, a plea, a dodge, a test. Before writing it,
know what the speaker wants, what is in the way, and what they try. Name the try with a verb for
what the line does, never for what it says. If no verb fits, the line is only talk. The next line
answers that act and not its topic: it gives way, blocks, turns it aside or hits back, and it is
rarely the answer the speaker hoped for. When a try fails the speaker tries something else, and
those changes are what move a scene. A line that only informs, a line said to the audience, and a
report where a person would talk are none of them acts. Nobody speaks for long unanswered: a
listener's silence or look is an answer, and it changes what the speaker does next. Ask what this
line is doing to the person it is said to, what they do back, and where the speaker changes what
they are trying.

**The unsaid: what matters most is held back.** People seldom say what they most want or feel.
They come at it through something else: the job in hand, an object, a joke, someone who isn't
there. McKee calls it the third thing. For each speaker, settle what they know and won't say, and
what they couldn't put into words if they tried. Write only what they would say aloud, shaped so
the audience can sense the rest. What holds a speaker back is what they stand to lose with this
listener, so the more they could lose, the less they say. People with a history together say
little, and only a stranger needs things spelled out. Nobody names their own feeling, explains
themselves with a counsellor's insight, or announces what they have learned. Keep the words
smaller than the feeling: a line bigger than its cause is melodrama, and the cure is a bigger
cause or a smaller line. A plain statement lands only when what came before has loaded it. A piece
told as fable, farce or fairy tale may speak plainly, so settle which kind this is. Ask what each
speaker wants and won't say, what they are talking about instead, and which line names a feeling
outright.

**Only theirs: a line no one else in the cast could say.** A voice comes from what a character
knows and how they stand toward the world and the person in front of them. What they know shows in
what they call things: the exact word or the vague one, their own word or someone else's. How they
stand shows in how they qualify: orders or questions, must or might, plain claims or hedges, and
how long they hold the floor. People lose words under pressure, and whoever is in control talks
longer and slower. A character talks differently to each listener. A voice is not a catchphrase or
an accent laid over lines that are otherwise alike. The first line that comes to mind is usually
one any character in any game could say, so write it down, then look for the one only this
character would say here. Ask who is speaking when the names are covered, which word here is
theirs alone, and how they would say it to someone else.

**Facts as ammunition: a fact comes out because someone uses it.** Sort what the audience has to
know into what they need now, what can wait and what they never need. Give only the first, one
fact at a time, as late as they can still follow. A character says a fact in order to get
something with it: to hurt, to win someone over, to defend themselves. Nobody tells another what
both already know, though an outsider can honestly be told. A fact lands when the audience already
wants it, so raise the question before answering it. The secrets that matter most are kept for a
turn, and come out only when silence would cost more than telling. No one speaks with knowledge
only the writer has. What a later scene will need is planted earlier, in passing. The one
exception is what the player needs in order to play: where to go and what has changed are said
plainly, and kept where they can be found again. Ask who in the scene needs this fact now and what
they do with it, whether the listener already knows it, and whether it could wait or be seen
instead.

**Heard once: built for the mouth, the ear and the caption.** A spoken line goes by once, at the
speed of speech, to someone who is also watching. It has to make sense at first hearing: familiar
words, the usual order, one thought to a line, and few invented names. Put the word the line turns
on at the end, so the meaning lands there and the answer can come in on it. A line that trails on
past its point makes the answer wait. Vary the shapes, since the same build every time sounds like
a machine. Cut greetings, small talk, names said for no reason, anything a look has already
answered, and whatever follows the last line the scene needs. Say every line aloud, and rewrite
what trips the tongue, repeats a sound by accident or needs a second breath. Then count. Speech
runs two to three words a second, and the picture needs room of its own. A caption holds two rows
of about 40 characters, so a longer line is split at a breath or cut. A project's own limits
replace these numbers. Where a line needs a note to be read right, fix the words first. In a
project that is translated, flag any line whose point rests on a pun, a rhyme or an idiom. Ask
whether it can be said in one breath and read in one caption, where its meaning lands, and what
can go with nothing lost.

## Lines in play

**Heard a hundred times: lines the player hears while playing.** Mission lines over the action,
barks set off by what happens, and the chatter and banter that fill a world all reach a player
whose hands and eyes are busy. Keep each to one idea in few words, with the point in the first
words, because the rest may be lost. Nothing the player must have lives only in a line they can
miss: put it on the screen as well, or let it come round again. Say what the player can't see for
themselves: where it is, how near, what is about to happen. A line that only announces its own
trigger, or reports what they just watched or what the interface already shows, is noise. Nothing
gets explained during a fight: keep talk for the lulls. A line can say only what its trigger knows,
so find out what the game can tell it (which side, how far, who) and write the versions to that.
Every one of these lines will be heard again, so write several versions for each trigger, and more
for the triggers that fire most. The more often a line fires, the plainer it should be: a plain
call can come round again and sound like procedure, and a joke, a nickname or a turn of phrase
can't. Keep those for the triggers that fire rarely. Each version is still only theirs, which in
three words means the words they would pick. A line must be true whenever it fires, so check each
version against every state it can play in. Play cuts lines off, so make them able to stop
cleanly, and to pick up again or be dropped. Not every event needs a line. Ask whether a busy
player can take it in and lose nothing by missing it, what it tells them that they couldn't see,
how it sounds the hundredth time, and whether it is true in every state where it can fire.

## Words come last

Plan and write in this order, and go back up it when something fails:

1. The case for the cutscene, and what the player does on either side of it.
2. The turn: what is at stake, and how it stands at the start and at the end.
3. The beats, with no lines yet. For each: the picture, the act and its answer as two verbs, and
   what the audience should feel. No feeling runs three beats in a row, because repeated it wears
   out.
4. What each speaker holds back, and where each fact the audience needs comes out.
5. The words. Write each line from inside its speaker: in their place, wanting what they want,
   what would I say? Then read the scene as someone meeting it for the first time.
6. Three passes: each voice in turn; each line against its own picture, cutting the words that
   repeat it; then aloud, with the count.

When a line won't come right, the fault is usually above it: in the act, the beat or the turn. Go
up before rewording.

## Fit and size

Use the tests that fit the piece, and say in one line which you set aside and why. A cutscene
takes the first eight. A line polish takes the five tests of the lines. A set of barks takes
Heard a hundred times, Only theirs and Heard once. Check wants and a turn before any redraft,
however small the request.

Don't force them. A hidden meaning under every line makes riddles, and a voice pushed too far
makes a cast of oddities. Cutting every line to the bone leaves one terse voice for the whole
cast, so a talker stays a talker inside the seconds they have. Most good lines are short, ordinary
words doing one clear thing.

Scale each test to the piece, from a long cinematic down to one bark, or to a shorter cut of a
cutscene for a trailer or an ad, which keeps one turn and the lines that carry it. Lines shown in
text boxes take the same tests: the player sets the pace, and the box takes the caption's place.
A cutscene told in stills with voices takes them too. Follow the project's own script form, its
rating, and its rules on rough language.

## Story facts, voices, and what is already made

Read the project's documents first: the story so far, the character sheets, recorded decisions,
the script itself, and what the player has done and knows by this point. Find out what a cutscene
in this game can show: full animation or stills with voices, faces that act or faces that don't.
When diagnosing, you may challenge a recorded decision; present that as a named finding for the
user to accept or reject. When writing, recorded decisions bind: keep to them and flag the clash.

What is already made binds a redraft. Painted or animated pictures, recorded lines and cut timings
stay as they are unless the user says otherwise, so a redraft changes words inside them. A better
line that would need a new picture, a new take or a new timing is reported apart from the redraft,
with what it would cost to redo.

When a speaker has no sheet, infer a voice from the lines and the story there are: what they know,
how they qualify, what they won't say, and what pressure does to their speech. Say so in one line,
carry on, and report the gap. Never write story or character facts into the project's documents
unasked. When the work is an interview with the user, raise both cases as questions instead.

## What to hand back

- **Diagnosis:** the weak points, ordered by how much each hurts. For each, say where it is (the
  beat or the line), which test it fails, what the audience or the player loses, and the change:
  for a line, the redrafted line itself. Give no scores, and leave the script as it is.
- **Plan:** why this is a cutscene and where it sits in play; the turn; the beats as a table of
  picture, act and answer, and what the audience should feel; what each speaker holds back; and
  where each needed fact comes out.
- **Draft:** the script in the project's own form. Where it has none, two tables: the scene, a row
  for each beat with its time, its picture and the lines heard over it; and the lines, each with
  an ID, who says it, whether they are on screen, and what the line does. After the script, give
  up to two alternates for the few lines the scene turns on, five lines at most, each with a
  clause on what it changes. Then at most five lines on where each test is met or set aside, with
  the word count against the running time and any line over a caption. Keep the alternates and
  that note in the conversation and out of the script file, unless the project keeps them there.
- **Redraft in place:** the script with only its lines changed, then a table of each changed line
  before and after with the reason. Apart from it, list anything better that the fixed pictures or
  timings ruled out.
