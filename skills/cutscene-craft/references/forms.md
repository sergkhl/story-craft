# Forms: the tables, the counting, and the note a voice needs

Read this when drafting, redrafting or writing barks for a project that has no script form of its
own. Where the project has one, use it. The samples below show a form. Never borrow their content.

## The scene and the lines

A draft is two tables. The scene has a row for each beat:

| # | Time | Picture | Lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–4 s | The ferry is already pulling out. Ana runs the length of the pier. | — |
| 2 | 4–9 s | Her brother at the rail, not waving. | ANA: You left the keys! |

- **Time** is seconds from the start. Until a voice exists it is an estimate from the count below,
  and the draft says so.
- **Picture** says what happens, who is in it and what must be noticed. It names no lens, angle or
  cut.
- **Lines** holds the lines heard over that picture, or a dash. A row with a dash isn't empty: the
  picture is carrying the beat.

The lines have a row each, in the order they are heard:

| ID | Who | Heard | What the line does | Line |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Ana | on screen | Shouting across the water. She makes it about the keys so it isn't about him leaving. | You left the keys! |

- **ID** is a letter or two for the speaker and a number in the order they speak: A1, A2, B1. A
  redrafted line keeps its ID, a new line takes the next number, and a cut line's ID is not used
  again. Recording, captions and translation all hang on it.
- **Heard** is "on screen" or "voice only": over a radio, from out of shot, or in someone's head.
- **What the line does** is the act, the person it is aimed at, and what has just happened. It is
  also the note for whoever voices the line. Game lines are recorded out of order and often alone,
  so the note has to carry what the scene would have told the actor. Say what the speaker is doing
  and what they are covering, and leave the sound to the actor. Add how to say it only where the
  words could be read two ways. Give the pronunciation of any invented name, and any fact that
  changes the performance and can't be seen in the line, such as that the speaker is lying. When a
  line is redrafted, redraft its note.

## The plan

A plan lays out the beats before any line is written:

| # | Picture | Act and answer | The audience feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The ferry is already pulling out. Ana runs the length of the pier. | Chasing / leaving | alarm |
| 2 | Her brother at the rail, not waving. | Accusing / refusing to look | dread |

"Act and answer" is two verbs: what one person does and what comes back. The answer may be a look,
a silence or a thing done. Two rows with the same pair of verbs are one beat written twice. No
feeling runs three rows in a row.

## Counting

Count before the note that closes a draft, and put the numbers in it.

- **Words to seconds.** Speech runs two to three words a second. Use two and a half for ordinary
  talk, nearer two for slow or heavy lines, and nearer three for fast ones.
- **Room for the picture.** A cutscene's lines never fill its whole length. Pauses and the beats
  the picture carries alone need their seconds too. When the lines by themselves need more than
  the running time, cut lines or say that the scene has to be longer.
- **Captions.** A caption holds two rows of about 40 characters, counting letters, spaces and
  punctuation. A line over 80 characters is two captions, split where the speaker would breathe,
  or it is cut.
- **What to report.** The words in the lines, the seconds they need, the running time, and any
  line longer than one caption.

A project's own limits replace these.

## Alternates

Offer them only for the few lines the scene turns on, five lines at most, two alternates each:

| ID | The draft's line | B | C | What each changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|

An alternate is a different act or a different way of holding back. The same line in other words
isn't one.

## A redraft in place

When pictures, recordings or timings are already made, hand back the script with only its lines
changed, then:

| ID | Before | Redraft | Why |
|---|---|---|---|

Each redraft has to fit the time its line already has: the seconds of its slot, at three words a
second at most. Read each one against the picture in its own row, and cut the words that repeat
that picture: a fixed picture has already told the audience what it shows. After the table, list
apart what the fixed pictures and timings ruled out: the better line, what it would need, and what
that would cost to redo.

## Barks and other lines in play

| Trigger | Who | How often it fires | What the game can tell the line | Versions | Must be true when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- **How often it fires** decides how many versions there are and how plain they are. A trigger
  that fires many times an hour needs the most versions and the plainest: a call of a few words
  that can come round again like procedure. One that fires once in a session can carry a longer
  line, a joke or a nickname.
- **What the game can tell the line** is what the line is able to say: a side, a distance, a name,
  a count. A line that says where and how near is worth more than one that only announces. Where
  the brief doesn't offer these, ask for them, or write a version for each case and say that the
  game has to pick between them.
- **Must be true when** lists the states the line can fire in: alone or in company, first run or
  fiftieth, winning or losing. A version that is false in one of them is cut or given its own
  trigger.
- Where the brief doesn't say how often a trigger fires, whether the game holds a line back after
  it has played, or whether a line can be cut off, ask. If nobody can say, state what you assumed.
- When something the player needs is asked to live in a bark, write the bark and say where else
  it has to appear.

## Lines that will be translated

When the project is translated:

- Flag any line whose point rests on a pun, a rhyme or an idiom, and offer a plain version beside
  it.
- Don't build a line out of parts, such as a name or a number dropped into a sentence. Other
  languages order and inflect them differently.
- Leave room in the time and in the caption, since a translation can run longer than the original.
- Give the translator the same note the actor gets.
