# Techniques: how each test fails, and where to see it work

Read this when a test's verdict is unclear, or when the user asks why a finding matters. The works
named below show a mechanism. Never borrow their content, tone, or lines.

These notes give away turns in The Last of Us, Cowboy Bebop, Attack on Titan, Death Note, Neon
Genesis Evangelion, and Frieren: Beyond Journey's End.

## Earn the cutscene

It fails when:

- It shows the player's character doing what the player does all game: the fight, the jump, the
  shot. The best moment went to the one person who couldn't take part.
- It takes back what the player just did. The boss they beat gets up, or the character who won the
  fight is caught in the next shot.
- The character wants something the player has been given no reason to want, or does what no
  player would choose.
- It carries a briefing: minutes of names, places and orders before the player has touched
  anything.
- The only place an objective is given is a cutscene that can be skipped.
- It lands where the player wants to act, or straight after another cutscene.
- It retells what the level already showed.
- It plays again unchanged every time the player comes back to this point.
- An opening leaves the player not knowing what to do when the controls come back. A film's
  audience will wait to be told, and a player can't.

See it work:

- *The Last of Us*: the opening is played. The player walks Sarah through the dark house, looks
  around from the back seat of the car, then carries her as Joel. The controls go only for the
  moment that has to happen one way.
- *Portal 2*: almost nothing stops the play. GLaDOS, Wheatley and Cave Johnson's old recordings
  tell the story while the player is inside a test.
- *God of War* (2018): scenes and play share one unbroken shot, so no cut tells the player that
  their part is over.
- *Metal Gear Solid 2*: the cautionary case. Skolnick holds up its opening for the amount of talk
  and briefing a player sits through before they play.

A player watching a cutscene runs a timer in their head, as Brian Kindregan puts it in The Game
Narrative Toolbox. When it runs out they skip this cutscene, and probably every one after it.

## Picture first

It fails when:

- A line says what the picture shows: "He's getting away!" over the shot of him getting away.
- One character reports to another what the audience has just watched. The other needed telling,
  and the audience didn't.
- A character describes what they are doing while they do it, or says they can't believe what
  everyone just watched.
- A spoken yes, no or thanks where a nod or a look would do.
- Every beat has a line, so the picture is never alone.
- The lines were written with no picture in mind and would play the same on the radio.
- Pauses are everywhere, so none of them counts.
- The picture asks more of a face than the game's characters can show. Then the hands, the staging
  or a line has to carry it.

See it work:

- *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, episode 22: Asuka and Rei ride a lift for most of a minute in one
  unmoving shot before either speaks. The line that ends it lands on everything the silence held.
- *Cowboy Bebop*, "Ballad of Fallen Angels": the fall from the cathedral window has no line at
  all, only a song and what Spike sees.
- *Attack on Titan*, "Warrior": the confession is said in the back of the shot while the picture
  looks elsewhere. Line and picture pull apart, and the audience has to catch it.

## Wants and a turn

It fails when:

- Two people who agree trade information.
- The scene ends where it began. Nothing at stake has changed, and it could be cut without the
  story noticing.
- The turn comes in the first exchange and the rest is aftermath, or so late that the audience got
  there first.
- The same exchange comes round again in new words: he asks, she refuses; he asks, she refuses.
- Three beats in a row aim at the same feeling.
- The lines have been reworded over and over while the scene under them stayed the same.

See it work:

- *Death Note*, "Encounter": at the entrance ceremony L tells Light who he is. Until that line
  Light is the one who can't be seen. After it he is being watched from the next seat.
- *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, "Angel Attack": nothing that is said to Shinji moves him. What turns
  him is Rei, wheeled in hurt, and her blood on his hand. The turn is an act, and his line only
  reports a decision the audience has watched him make.

## Act and answer

It fails when:

- A line only informs. Take it out and nothing between the two people changes.
- Each speaker announces and nobody answers. The lines are addressed to the audience.
- A report where a person would talk: three status calls in a row.
- Every question gets a full, straight answer.
- One person talks for half a minute and the other might as well not be there.
- A try that keeps failing is tried again unchanged.
- A line answers the topic and not the act: she dares him, and he supplies a fact.

See it work:

- *Death Note*, "Encounter": L's announcement isn't news for Light. It is a trap laid in a public
  place, where Light can do nothing about it and has to answer with a straight face.
- *Cowboy Bebop*, the last episode: Faye stops Spike at gunpoint, and he answers with a story
  about his eyes. She is trying to keep him, he is telling her why he can't stay, and neither says
  so.
- *Portal 2*: everything GLaDOS says over the speakers is a try at something: needling, stalling,
  bargaining. None of it is announcement, though it comes from an announcer.

## The unsaid

It fails when:

- A character names their feeling: "I'm scared", "I'm so angry with you".
- Two people face each other and take turns stating their position on the thing between them.
- A character explains their own wound with a counsellor's insight.
- A character says what they have learned, or the last line says what the scene was about.
- The line is bigger than its cause: shouting, tears and vows over little.
- Every line hints and nothing can be followed. Holding something back needs something plain on
  top to hold it under.

See it work:

- *The Last of Us*: the last exchange. Ellie asks Joel to swear that his account is true. He
  swears, and she answers with one word. Whether she believes him is left to the player.
- *Cowboy Bebop*, the last episode: Spike tells Jet a story about a cat that died a million times.
  Jet says it's a good story. Spike says he hates cats. That is their goodbye, and neither of them
  says goodbye.
- *Frieren: Beyond Journey's End*, episode 7: Himmel gives vanity as his reason for all the
  statues. The other reason, that she shouldn't be alone later, comes out once and lightly.

Some pieces are exempt. McKee sets aside whole kinds of story where characters are types who mean
what they say: myth, fairy tale, farce, allegory, and much of fantasy and action. Decide by how
the piece asks to be taken, not by its setting. A drama set among robots is still a drama.

## Only theirs

It fails when:

- A stock line any cast could say: "Talk to me!", "We don't have much time.", "Stay with me!",
  "It's quiet. Too quiet.", "We've got company!"
- Everyone has the writer's voice: the same length, the same wit, whole sentences.
- Everyone banters, with the same quick joke under fire.
- Voice by tic: a catchphrase or an accent on lines that are otherwise alike.
- A character uses words they wouldn't have. The mechanic's line could be the scientist's.
- A character uses the game's own names for its systems where a person would use their own word.
- A character sounds the same to their commander as to their sister.

See it work:

- *Portal 2*: GLaDOS, Wheatley and Cave Johnson. Cover the names and nobody could mix them up: one
  needles in a clinical calm, one rambles and corrects himself, one sells.
- *Cowboy Bebop*: Jet lectures, Faye bargains, Ed sings nonsense about Ed, and Spike shrugs.
- *God of War* (2018): Kratos gets a word or two where others get a sentence, which is why his few
  longer speeches land.

## Facts as ammunition

It fails when:

- "As you know": someone tells another what both of them know.
- An interview: one person asks short questions so that the other can explain.
- The fact comes before anyone wants it. The audience has no question yet.
- Everything is laid out at the start.
- A character knows what only the writer could.
- History and names that no choice in the scene turns on.
- A secret is told at no cost to the teller.
- What the player needs in order to play is said once, in a line they can miss.

See it work:

- *Attack on Titan*, "Warrior": the largest fact in the story so far comes out as an offer. Reiner
  tells Eren who they are in order to get him to come along.
- *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, "Angel Attack": nobody explains what an Angel is or what an Eva is
  for. Shinji is told only as much as it takes to ask him to get in.
- *Portal 2*: the company's past arrives as Cave Johnson's old recordings, a man selling his tests
  to whoever stood in the room that day. Nobody narrates it.
- *Metal Gear Solid 2*: the cautionary case again. What the player has to know comes first and all
  together.

## Heard once

It fails when:

- A line needs reading twice: a clause inside a clause, abstract nouns, the point in the middle.
- A line crowded with invented names, which nobody can follow by ear.
- The point comes early and the line trails on, so the answer has to wait.
- "Well,", "Look," or "Listen," at the head of lines, and a name in every line.
- It reads well and can't be said: a run of s sounds, an accidental rhyme, no place to breathe.
- A line is longer than its shot, or than one caption.
- Every line has the same build and the same length.
- Written speech: "I am unable to" for "I can't", whole sentences, nobody cut off.
- The words can't be read right without "(sarcastically)".
- A point that rests on wordplay, in a game that will be translated.

See it work:

- *Cowboy Bebop*: the series ends on a single syllable.
- *The Last of Us*: the game ends on a single word, and it is an answer.

A comic line takes the same test harder. It has to be clear before it is clever, the word that
gets the laugh comes last with nothing after it, and the audience is kept far enough from the pain
to laugh.

## Heard a hundred times

It fails when:

- One line for a trigger: the same shout every time an enemy sees the player.
- The funny one: a joke, a nickname or a running bit on a trigger that fires twice a minute.
- A bark that only announces its trigger: "Fire!" for a fire the player was just shown, with
  nothing on where it is or how near.
- A bark that reports what the player just did, or what the interface already shows: time to
  reload, health is low.
- Explaining during a fight. Nobody is listening, and the line drowns under the shouting.
- Something the player needs is said once, during a fight.
- A line that is false in some state: "We're all here" after one of them went down, or a welcome
  for a newcomer on the fiftieth run.
- A long line over the action, or two lines on top of each other.
- Chatter that starts again from the top after every interruption, or carries on about something
  already over.
- Every character has the same bark in a different voice.

See it work:

- *Hades*: written for the fiftieth return. Each time the player dies and comes home, the house
  has something new to say, and Hypnos alone has dozens of remarks on what killed them. With more
  than twenty thousand voiced lines, a repeat is rare.
- *God of War* (2018): the stories told in the boat stop when the player steps ashore, and are
  taken up again when they are back aboard.
- *The Last of Us*: some talk is the player's to ask for. A prompt appears, and the conversation
  happens only if they take it.
